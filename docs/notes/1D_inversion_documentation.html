1D Magnetotelluric Inversion: Mathematical Documentation

1. Introduction to Magnetotellurics (MT)

Magnetotellurics is an electromagnetic geophysical method that uses natural variations in the Earth's magnetic and electric fields to determine subsurface electrical resistivity structure. The method is based on the diffusion of electromagnetic waves through conductive media.

1.1 The Impedance Tensor

The fundamental quantity in MT is the impedance tensor \(\mathbf{Z}\), which relates the horizontal electric fields to the horizontal magnetic fields in the frequency domain:

$$\begin{bmatrix} E_x \\ E_y \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} Z_{xx} & Z_{xy} \\ Z_{yx} & Z_{yy} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} H_x \\ H_y \end{bmatrix}$$

For a 1D (horizontally layered) Earth structure, the diagonal elements are zero (\(Z_{xx} = Z_{yy} = 0\)), and we have:

$$E_x = Z_{xy} H_y \quad \text{and} \quad E_y = Z_{yx} H_x$$

1.2 Apparent Resistivity and Phase

The apparent resistivity is derived from the impedance magnitude:

$$\rho_a = \frac{1}{\omega \mu_0} |Z|^2 = \frac{|Z|^2}{2\pi f \mu_0}$$

where:

  • \(\omega = 2\pi f\) is the angular frequency (rad/s)
  • \(f\) is the frequency (Hz)
  • \(\mu_0 = 4\pi \times 10^{-7}\) H/m is the magnetic permeability of free space
  • \(|Z|\) is the magnitude of the impedance

For standard EDI files with impedance in [mV/km]/[nT], the formula simplifies to:

$$\rho_a = \frac{|Z|^2}{5f}$$

1.3 Skin Depth

The skin depth represents the characteristic depth to which electromagnetic signals penetrate:

$$\delta = \sqrt{\frac{\rho}{\pi f \mu_0}} \approx 503 \sqrt{\frac{\rho}{f}} \quad \text{(in meters)}$$

This relationship is fundamental: lower frequencies penetrate deeper.

2. The Bostick Transformation

2.1 Theoretical Foundation

The Bostick transformation (Bostick, 1977) provides a rapid approximate inversion method that converts apparent resistivity curves directly into a 1D resistivity-depth model without formal iterative inversion.

Key Assumption: The transformation assumes that the apparent resistivity measured at a given period is primarily influenced by the resistivity at the corresponding skin depth.

2.2 Mathematical Derivation

The Bostick transformation relates the true resistivity \(\rho(z)\) at depth \(z\) to the apparent resistivity \(\rho_a(T)\) and its derivative with respect to period \(T\):

$$\rho_{\text{Bostick}}(z) = \rho_a(T) \left[ 1 + \frac{T}{2\rho_a} \frac{d\rho_a}{dT} \right]$$

This can be rewritten in terms of logarithmic derivatives:

$$\rho_{\text{Bostick}}(z) = \rho_a(T) \left[ 1 + \frac{d \ln \rho_a}{d \ln T} \right]$$

The corresponding depth is given by:

$$z_{\text{Bostick}} = \frac{\delta}{\sqrt{2}} = \frac{503}{\sqrt{2}} \sqrt{\frac{\rho_a}{f}} = 356 \sqrt{\frac{\rho_a}{f}}$$

2.3 Implementation Details

Algorithm: Bostick Transformation
  1. Input: Apparent resistivity \(\rho_a(f)\) vs frequency \(f\)
  2. Convert frequency to period: \(T = 1/f\)
  3. Calculate average apparent resistivity: \(\rho_{\text{avg}} = \sqrt{\rho_{xy} \cdot \rho_{yx}}\)
  4. Compute logarithmic derivative:
    $$\frac{d \ln \rho_a}{d \ln T} = \frac{\nabla(\ln \rho_a)}{\nabla(\ln T)}$$
    where \(\nabla\) denotes numerical gradient
  5. Apply derivative limiting to avoid extreme values:
    $$\text{deriv}_{\text{limited}} = \text{clip}\left(\frac{d \ln \rho_a}{d \ln T}, -0.8, 2.0\right)$$
  6. Calculate Bostick resistivity:
    $$\rho_{\text{Bostick}} = \rho_a (1 + \text{deriv}_{\text{limited}})$$
  7. Calculate Bostick depth:
    $$z_{\text{Bostick}} = \frac{503}{\sqrt{2}} \sqrt{\frac{\rho_a}{f}}$$
  8. Output: Resistivity-depth pairs \((\rho_{\text{Bostick}}, z_{\text{Bostick}})\)

2.4 Code Implementation

# Calculate average apparent resistivity (geometric mean) rho_avg = np.sqrt(rho_xy * rho_yx) # Calculate skin depth depth = 503 * np.sqrt(rho_avg / freq) # Calculate logarithmic derivative d_log_rho = np.gradient(np.log(rho_avg)) d_log_T = np.gradient(np.log(periodo)) deriv = d_log_rho / d_log_T # Apply Bostick transformation with derivative limiting deriv_limited = np.clip(deriv, -0.8, 2.0) rho_bostick = rho_avg * (1 + deriv_limited) # Ensure positive values rho_bostick = np.maximum(rho_bostick, 1.0) # Calculate Bostick depth depth_bostick = depth / np.sqrt(2)

2.5 Limitations of Bostick Transformation

  • Local approximation: Assumes smooth variations in resistivity
  • No optimization: Not a least-squares fit to the data
  • Derivative sensitivity: Sensitive to noise in the data
  • 1D assumption: Breaks down for 2D/3D structures
  • No uncertainty estimates: Cannot provide error bounds

3. 1D Forward Modeling: Wait's Recursion Algorithm

3.1 The Forward Problem

Given a 1D layered Earth model with \(N\) layers, each characterized by resistivity \(\rho_i\) and thickness \(h_i\), the forward problem is to calculate the surface impedance \(Z_0\) and hence the apparent resistivity that would be observed.

3.2 Maxwell's Equations in 1D

For a horizontally layered Earth with plane wave electromagnetic fields, Maxwell's equations reduce to:

$$\frac{d^2 E}{dz^2} = k^2 E$$

where the propagation constant is:

$$k = \sqrt{i \omega \mu_0 / \rho} = (1+i) \sqrt{\pi f \mu_0 / \rho}$$

3.3 Impedance in a Single Layer

The intrinsic impedance of layer \(j\) is:

$$Z_0^{(j)} = \sqrt{i \omega \mu_0 \rho_j} = (1+i) \sqrt{\pi f \mu_0 \rho_j}$$

3.4 Wait's Recursion Formula

Wait (1954, 1962) developed a recursive algorithm to compute the surface impedance by starting from the bottom layer (half-space) and propagating upward through each layer.

Key Concept: The impedance at the top of layer \(j\) depends on the impedance at the bottom of layer \(j\) and the layer properties.

The recursion formula is:

$$Z_j = Z_0^{(j)} \frac{1 + r_j e^{-2k_j h_j}}{1 - r_j e^{-2k_j h_j}}$$

where the reflection coefficient at the interface is:

$$r_j = \frac{Z_{j+1} - Z_0^{(j)}}{Z_{j+1} + Z_0^{(j)}}$$

Here:

  • \(Z_j\) is the impedance looking down from the top of layer \(j\)
  • \(Z_{j+1}\) is the impedance looking down from the top of layer \(j+1\) (previously calculated)
  • \(Z_0^{(j)}\) is the intrinsic impedance of layer \(j\)
  • \(k_j\) is the propagation constant in layer \(j\)
  • \(h_j\) is the thickness of layer \(j\)

3.5 Complete Algorithm

Algorithm: Wait's Recursion for 1D MT Forward Modeling
  1. Input:
    • Periods \(T_1, T_2, \ldots, T_M\)
    • Layer resistivities \(\rho_1, \rho_2, \ldots, \rho_N\)
    • Layer thicknesses \(h_1, h_2, \ldots, h_{N-1}\) (bottom layer is half-space)
  2. For each period \(T_i\):
    1. Calculate angular frequency: \(\omega = 2\pi / T_i\)
    2. Initialize with half-space (bottom layer \(N\)):
      $$Z_N = Z_0^{(N)} = \sqrt{i \omega \mu_0 \rho_N}$$
    3. Recursion upward from layer \(N-1\) to layer 1:
      • For layer \(j = N-1, N-2, \ldots, 1\):
      • Calculate propagation constant:
        $$k_j = \sqrt{i \omega \mu_0 / \rho_j}$$
      • Calculate intrinsic impedance:
        $$Z_0^{(j)} = \sqrt{i \omega \mu_0 \rho_j}$$
      • Calculate reflection coefficient:
        $$r_j = \frac{Z_{j+1} - Z_0^{(j)}}{Z_{j+1} + Z_0^{(j)}}$$
      • Update impedance:
        $$Z_j = Z_0^{(j)} \frac{1 + r_j e^{-2k_j h_j}}{1 - r_j e^{-2k_j h_j}}$$
    4. Calculate apparent resistivity at surface:
      $$\rho_{a,i} = \frac{|Z_1|^2}{\omega \mu_0}$$
  3. Output: Synthetic apparent resistivities \(\rho_{a,1}, \rho_{a,2}, \ldots, \rho_{a,M}\)

3.6 Code Implementation

def mt_1d_forward(periods, resistivities, thicknesses): """ Calculate 1D MT forward response using Wait's recursion Parameters: periods: array of periods (s) resistivities: array of layer resistivities (Ohm*m) thicknesses: array of layer thicknesses (m) Returns: rho_app: apparent resistivities """ mu = 4 * np.pi * 1e-7 # Magnetic permeability rho_app = np.zeros(len(periods)) for i, T in enumerate(periods): omega = 2 * np.pi / T # Start from bottom layer (half-space) n_layers = len(resistivities) Z = np.sqrt(1j * omega * mu * resistivities[-1]) # Recursion from bottom to top for j in range(n_layers - 2, -1, -1): rho_j = resistivities[j] h_j = thicknesses[j] # Propagation constant k_j = np.sqrt(1j * omega * mu / rho_j) # Intrinsic impedance Z0_j = np.sqrt(1j * omega * mu * rho_j) # Reflection coefficient r = (Z - Z0_j) / (Z + Z0_j) # Update impedance Z = Z0_j * (1 + r * np.exp(-2 * k_j * h_j)) / \ (1 - r * np.exp(-2 * k_j * h_j)) # Calculate apparent resistivity rho_app[i] = (1 / (omega * mu)) * np.abs(Z)**2 return rho_app

3.7 Numerical Considerations

  • Complex arithmetic: All calculations use complex numbers due to \(i = \sqrt{-1}\)
  • Exponential stability: For large \(k_j h_j\), \(e^{-2k_j h_j} \to 0\), ensuring numerical stability
  • Layer discretization: Model is discretized into ~30 layers to balance accuracy and computation
  • Bottom half-space: Last layer has effectively infinite thickness

4. Data Quality Control: Filtering Anomalous Frequencies

4.1 Why Filter?

Raw MT data can contain outliers due to:

  • Cultural electromagnetic noise (power lines, trains, etc.)
  • Near-field source effects
  • Processing artifacts
  • 3D effects in nominally 1D interpretation

4.2 Filtering Criteria

The code applies three filters to identify and remove anomalous data points:

Filter 1: Validity Check
$$\text{valid} = \text{isfinite}(\rho_{xy}) \land \text{isfinite}(\rho_{yx})$$

Removes NaN (Not a Number) and Inf (Infinity) values.

Filter 2: Physical Range
$$0.1 \leq \rho_{\text{avg}} \leq 10000 \quad \text{(Ohm·m)}$$

Removes unphysical resistivity values. Most crustal rocks have resistivities between 1 and 10,000 Ohm·m.

Filter 3: TE-TM Consistency
$$0.1 \leq \frac{\rho_{xy}}{\rho_{yx}} \leq 10$$

In 1D, TE and TM modes should give similar resistivities. Large differences indicate 2D/3D effects or bad data.

4.3 Implementation

# Create mask for valid data valid_mask = np.ones(len(freq), dtype=bool) # Remove NaN or Inf valid_mask &= np.isfinite(rho_xy) & np.isfinite(rho_yx) # Remove extreme values valid_mask &= (rho_avg > 0.1) & (rho_avg < 10000) # Remove frequencies where TE and TM differ by > factor of 10 ratio_te_tm = rho_xy / rho_yx valid_mask &= (ratio_te_tm > 0.1) & (ratio_te_tm < 10) # Apply filter freq_filtered = freq[valid_mask] rho_xy_filtered = rho_xy[valid_mask] rho_yx_filtered = rho_yx[valid_mask]

5. Model Assessment: RMS Misfit

5.1 Definition

The Root Mean Square (RMS) misfit quantifies how well the synthetic response from the model matches the observed data:

$$\text{RMS} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \left( \log_{10} \rho_{a,i}^{\text{obs}} - \log_{10} \rho_{a,i}^{\text{syn}} \right)^2}$$

where:

  • \(\rho_{a,i}^{\text{obs}}\) is the observed (measured) apparent resistivity
  • \(\rho_{a,i}^{\text{syn}}\) is the synthetic (calculated) apparent resistivity from the model
  • \(N\) is the number of data points

5.2 Why Logarithmic Scale?

Resistivity varies over many orders of magnitude (10⁰ to 10⁵ Ohm·m). Using logarithms:

  • Treats relative errors equally across all magnitudes
  • Standard practice in geophysics
  • A factor of 2 error has the same weight whether it's 10 vs 20 or 1000 vs 2000 Ohm·m

5.3 Interpretation Guidelines

RMS Misfit Model Quality Interpretation
< 0.3 Excellent Model fits data very well. Bostick approximation is highly accurate.
0.3 - 0.5 Good Acceptable fit. Minor discrepancies may be due to data noise or 1D approximation.
0.5 - 1.0 Moderate Model captures general trends but misses details. Consider formal inversion.
> 1.0 Poor Significant misfit. Structure may be 2D/3D, or data quality issues. Formal inversion recommended.

6. Complete Workflow Summary

Complete 1D MT Inversion and Forward Modeling Workflow
  1. Data Input
    • Read EDI file containing MT impedance data
    • Extract frequencies and impedance tensor components
  2. Quality Control
    • Apply validity, physical range, and TE-TM consistency filters
    • Remove \(n\) anomalous frequencies
  3. Calculate Apparent Resistivity
    • Compute \(\rho_{xy}\) and \(\rho_{yx}\) from impedance
    • Calculate geometric mean: \(\rho_{\text{avg}} = \sqrt{\rho_{xy} \cdot \rho_{yx}}\)
  4. Bostick Transformation (Approximate Inversion)
    • Calculate logarithmic derivative of apparent resistivity
    • Apply Bostick formula to get resistivity-depth model
    • Result: \((\rho_{\text{Bostick}}(z), z_{\text{Bostick}})\) pairs
  5. Model Discretization
    • Convert continuous Bostick model into ~30 layers
    • Define layer resistivities and thicknesses
  6. Forward Modeling
    • Use Wait's recursion to calculate synthetic response
    • For each period, compute surface impedance and apparent resistivity
  7. Model Validation
    • Compare observed (\(\rho_{\text{avg}}\)) vs synthetic (\(\rho_{\text{syn}}\)) curves
    • Calculate RMS misfit in logarithmic scale
    • Assess model quality based on misfit value
  8. Visualization
    • Plot 1: Observed TE and TM apparent resistivities
    • Plot 2: Observed average vs synthetic forward response
    • Plot 3: 1D resistivity-depth model from Bostick transformation

9. Conclusions and Recommendations

9.1 When to Use Bostick Transformation

  • Quick reconnaissance: Rapid first-look at subsurface structure
  • 1D structures: Horizontally layered environments (sedimentary basins)
  • Good data quality: Clean data with minimal noise
  • Initial model: Starting point for formal iterative inversion

9.2 When to Use Formal Inversion

  • High RMS misfit (> 0.5): Bostick approximation inadequate
  • Complex structures: Thin layers, sharp contrasts, or 2D/3D features
  • Quantitative interpretation: Need uncertainty estimates
  • Publication-quality results: Requires rigorous inversion

9.3 Advantages of the Implemented Approach

  • Fast computation (no iterative optimization)
  • No initial model required
  • Automatic quality control filtering
  • Forward modeling validation of results
  • Visual comparison of observed vs synthetic responses

9.4 Limitations and Caveats

  • Assumes perfect 1D structure (breaks down for 2D/3D)
  • Sensitive to data noise and outliers
  • No uncertainty quantification
  • Limited resolution for thin layers
  • Cannot handle sharp resistivity contrasts well
